# Interview 1 — Masum Choudhuri

**Role:** Software Engineer 2 at Bain & Company  
**Interview format:** LinkedIn chat  
**Date:** 12 May 2026

## Tools currently used
- Claude Code
- Enterprise AI tooling through organization workflows

## Daily-use tools
- Claude

## How spending is managed
- Enterprise AI expenditures are reviewed quarterly by the organization

## Usefulness of the audit tool
- Personal-level audit less useful
- Potentially useful for startups and smaller organizations

## Key insights
- Enterprise AI purchasing is often centralized and reviewed periodically
- Personal AI spending and organizational AI spending behave very differently
- Small teams are likely a better early target market than large enterprises

## Most important quote

> “At personal level, my usage of paid AI tools is still limited so an audit tool at personal level won't help much, but might be useful for small teams or organizations.”

## What this changed in my thinking

This interview clarified that the product’s strongest early users are probably not large enterprises. Bigger organizations already have procurement oversight, quarterly reviews, and internal approval processes.

The stronger opportunity appears to be:
- startups
- small engineering teams
- founders
- fast-moving product teams

where subscriptions grow organically without centralized visibility.

# Interview 2 — Ritupan Deka

**Role:** Junior Backend Developer at Vasp Technologies  
**Interview format:** LinkedIn chat  
**Date:** 13 May 2026

## Tools currently used
- Cursor
- Antigravity
- ChatGPT
- Gemini

## Paid or free?
Mostly free plans. No paid subscriptions yet.

## Daily-use tools
- Claude
- ChatGPT

## Key insights
- Team switches tools depending on the task
- AI mainly used for repetitive tasks, R&D, and image generation
- Developers still do not fully trust AI with complex logic-heavy workflows
- Team does not currently feel strong pressure to upgrade to paid plans

## Most important quote

> “As developers we do not trust AI to handle the logic part as it is too time consuming to explain the AI model about the flow of the projects or task.”

## What this changed in my thinking

Initially I assumed overspending would mainly come from unused paid subscriptions. This interview showed another pattern: many small teams are still experimenting on free tiers and switching between tools opportunistically instead of committing to one platform.

It also reinforced that the audit tool should not aggressively push upgrades or savings recommendations where none exist. Honest reporting builds more trust than forcing optimization suggestions.

# Overall patterns across interviews

## 1. AI tool usage is fragmented

Most users are not committed to a single AI platform. They switch tools depending on:
- coding
- research
- image generation
- experimentation
- repetitive workflows

---

## 2. Small teams behave differently from enterprises

Enterprise spending is structured and reviewed periodically. Smaller teams operate much more informally and are more likely to accumulate overlapping subscriptions.

---

## 3. Trust in AI is still limited

Even frequent AI users remain cautious about using AI for:
- core application logic
- architecture decisions
- complex workflows

AI is mainly trusted for:
- repetitive tasks
- prototyping
- research
- assistance

---

## 4. Honest recommendations matter

Not every user is overspending. Some users already review subscriptions carefully. That reinforced the decision to allow the audit tool to explicitly say:

> “You are already spending efficiently.”

instead of forcing optimization suggestions where none exist.

---

# Biggest takeaway

The interviews changed my assumption that the product was mainly a “cost-cutting” tool.

It is more accurately a:
- visibility tool
- subscription-awareness tool
- optimization assistant for fast-moving teams

The strongest positioning is probably not:

> “You are wasting money.”

but:

> “You may not realize where your AI stack overlaps.”
